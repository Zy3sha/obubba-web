Can My Baby Eat Cheese? What to Pick and How to Serve It

From around six months, babies can eat pasteurised full-fat cheese. A simple UK guide to choosing a cheese, serving it safely and introducing dairy.

You have a little cheese in your hand and suddenly the ordinary snack raises six questions: Is it too salty? Does it need cooking? Is cream cheese allowed? What about the milk-allergy warning?

From around six months, once your baby is ready for solids, you can offer pasteurised full-fat cheese. Mild cheddar, cottage cheese and cream cheese are examples the NHS names as suitable. Choose a lower-salt option when you can, grate it or cut it into short, narrow strips, and stay with your baby while they eat. Cheese is a food, not a substitute for their usual milk feeds.

This is general information for a baby starting solids in the UK. Follow an individual feeding or allergy plan from your baby's clinician.

The quick check at the fridge

Before offering cheese, ask:

  1. Is my baby ready for solids? Most babies show the NHS readiness signs at around six months: they can stay sitting and hold their head steady, coordinate looking and reaching, and swallow food rather than pushing it straight back out.
  2. Does the label say pasteurised? Check especially with farm-shop, imported or unfamiliar cheese.
  3. Is it full-fat and relatively low in salt? The NHS recommends full-fat dairy for children under two and advises against adding salt to babies' food. Compare labels if you have a choice; no cheese needs to be a daily target.
  4. Can I serve it safely? The NHS preparation guide says to grate cheese or cut it into short, narrow strips. Sit your baby upright and alert, and supervise the whole meal.

A tiny taste counts as exploring. There is no amount your baby has to finish.

Which cheeses can I offer?

Pasteurised, full-fat mild cheddar is one straightforward option. Cottage cheese and cream cheese made from pasteurised milk are also on the NHS list. You could offer a little grated cheese on soft cooked vegetables, or a small amount of plain cream cheese spread thinly on an appropriately prepared piece of toast. Let your baby handle the texture and decide whether to take more.

Do not give an unpasteurised cheese. The NHS foods-to-avoid guide also says babies and young children should avoid mould-ripened soft cheese such as brie or camembert, ripened goat's cheese such as chèvre, and soft blue-veined cheese such as roquefort. A pasteurised brie is still mould-ripened, so the word “pasteurised” alone does not make every cheese suitable. If you are unsure what a cheese is, choose a familiar pasteurised alternative rather than guessing.

For this first offer, skip big cubes or thick, chewy pieces. Food shape and texture matter as much as the ingredient. Our UK weaning foods-to-avoid guide covers other common shopping-list questions.

Is cheese a milk-allergen introduction?

Yes. Cheese contains milk protein. If milk is new to your baby, offer a small amount as the only new allergen in that meal, so it is easier to notice a reaction. The NHS food-allergy guidance advises introducing potentially allergenic foods from around six months, one at a time and in small amounts. If tolerated, keep them in the usual varied diet; you do not need to turn this into a test or a streak.

If your baby has a diagnosed food allergy, eczema, or a family history of allergic conditions, ask your GP or health visitor for advice before introducing allergens. If they have a known cow's-milk allergy, do not trial cheese at home unless their allergy team has specifically given you a plan. Cheese and plain full-fat yoghurt are both dairy foods; neither is a workaround for milk allergy.

If you notice a possible reaction, stop the food and seek medical advice. Call 999 immediately if your baby has breathing difficulty, tongue or throat swelling, becomes floppy or collapses; the NHS treats these as possible signs of anaphylaxis. Our allergen-introduction guide explains what details are useful to record, but a log is never a reason to delay urgent help.

But isn't cow's milk meant to wait until one?

The distinction is food versus main drink. The NHS first-solids guidance says pasteurised full-fat cheese can be given from around six months. Cow's milk can be mixed into food from then too, but should not replace breast milk or first infant formula as the main drink before 12 months. You do not need to change a milk feed because a little cheese was offered at lunch.

A low-pressure first try

At a meal when your baby is well and alert, put a small amount of grated pasteurised cheese beside a familiar soft food. Sit together. Watch how your baby handles it and let them stop when they want. If they spit it out, that is information about today's taste or texture—not a verdict on cheese forever. The first-foods-spitting guide has a calm next step for that moment.

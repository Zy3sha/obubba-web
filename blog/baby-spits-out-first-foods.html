Baby Spits Out Every First Food. Is It Too Soon?

If your baby pushes purée or soft food back out, check readiness, try one calm mealtime reset and learn when gagging or swallowing needs help.

You offered one little spoonful. Your baby looked at you, moved it around, and sent it straight back onto the bib. If that happens again tomorrow, it can feel as though weaning has already gone wrong.

A little food coming back out is common while a baby learns what to do with a new taste and texture. But if almost every taste is pushed straight out, check the signs of readiness too. The NHS says babies are usually ready for solids from around six months when three signs appear together: they can sit with a steady head, bring food to their mouth with coordinated eyes and hands, and swallow food rather than pushing it back out. One messy meal is not a diagnosis; a persistent pattern is worth discussing with your health visitor.

First ask: is this a new skill or a readiness question?

Look at the whole mealtime, not just the amount left on the bib. Is your baby alert and able to sit safely upright? Do they reach for food or open their mouth when it is offered? Can you see some food being swallowed, even if plenty comes back out?

The NHS describes learning to move food around the mouth and swallow as part of starting solids. NHS Healthier Together guidance says not to worry if a baby spits out foods at first; they may need several chances to try a new food. That is different from insisting that a baby who cannot yet swallow solids must keep practising with bigger spoonfuls.

If your baby is younger than around six months, or the readiness signs have not come together, speak to your health visitor about when to try again. If your baby was born prematurely, ask your health visitor or GP for individual timing advice. Do not start solids early because someone says it will help sleep; the NHS says it will not make a baby more likely to sleep through.

Try one easier meal, without trying to win the spoon

At the next opportunity, make the aim exploration, not an empty bowl:

  1. Choose a moment when your baby is awake and calm, not exhausted or desperately hungry. Keep their usual breast or formula feeds responsive.
  2. Seat them safely upright and stay with them. Offer a small amount of safely prepared food in a texture they can manage, then wait for them to show interest.
  3. If using a spoon, wait for an open mouth. Let your baby touch the food or hold a spoon if they want to. Give them time to move the taste around.
  4. When they close their mouth, turn away or push food aside, stop. You can offer another chance on another day; there is no need to scrape the food back in.

These steps follow the NHS advice to go at your baby's pace, allow time and never force a feed. Early on, how much is swallowed matters less than becoming familiar with food. Breast milk or first infant formula remains the main drink during the first year. You do not need a gram or spoonful target to make this meal count.

Was it a spit, a gag, or a choking sign?

Food quietly sliding back out of the mouth is not the same as gagging. During a gag, your baby may make a noise, retch, push their tongue forward or bring food back up while learning to manage it. The NHS explains gagging and choking separately: choking can be quiet and needs immediate action if your baby cannot breathe properly. Learn baby choking first aid before or as you begin solids; do not leave your baby alone with food.

If your baby repeatedly coughs or chokes with food or milk, has a wet or gurgly sound after eating, seems short of breath, or struggles to swallow, ask for an urgent GP appointment or contact NHS 111. Those are NHS swallowing-problem warning signs, not something to solve by changing purée thickness yourself. If your baby is choking and cannot breathe properly, follow NHS first-aid guidance and call 999 as directed. Seek prompt help too if milk feeds drop, wet nappies become fewer or your baby seems unwell.

Keep the record small enough to be useful

If you want to notice a pattern, write down the food and texture, whether your baby brought it to their mouth, and whether they swallowed any or pushed it out. “Mashed pear, interested, most came back out” is more useful than a percentage. You can take that short note to your health visitor if the difficulty continues.

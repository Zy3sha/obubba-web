My Baby Cries Halfway Through a Bottle. What Should I Try?

When a bottle feed turns into tears, pause the milk, check your baby's cues and know which feeding signs need help. You do not have to make them finish.

Your baby takes a few good sucks, then pulls away and cries. There is milk left, and you are wondering whether to keep offering it, try a different teat, or worry that something is wrong.

Pause the feed first. Bring the bottle down or remove the teat gently, hold your baby close, and give them a moment. A cry partway through a bottle does not tell you one cause. They may need a break, have had enough, be uncomfortable, or be finding the flow hard to manage. You do not need to persuade them to finish the bottle. The NHS advises following your baby's cues and never forcing the remainder.

A calm check in the moment

  1. Stop the milk flow. Keep your baby supported and watch their face and breathing. If they are coughing or spluttering, do not keep the milk flowing while trying to settle them.
  2. Give them a breather. They may want to burp or simply pause. The NHS says short breaks during a bottle feed are normal.
  3. Look for an invitation, not a target amount. If your baby settles and roots for the teat again, offer it gently and let them draw it in. Hold the bottle almost horizontal so the milk does not run too fast. If they turn away, close their mouth, push the bottle away or cry again, stop rather than repeatedly re-offering it.
  4. Notice the whole feed. Was there coughing, milk spilling from the mouth, a lot of gulping, bringing milk back up, or pain-looking distress? Did the crying happen once or across several feeds? Those observations are more useful than the unfinished number alone.

Do not prop a bottle or leave your baby feeding alone. The NHS bottle-feeding advice warns that a baby may choke when a bottle is propped. If the feed is over, follow that page's advice to discard unused milk from the bottle rather than saving the unfinished portion for later.

What might the pause mean?

Sometimes it is simply a normal pause or a fullness cue. The NHS responsive-feeding guide says babies often take a little, rest, then take more. A smaller feed today is not automatically a problem if your baby otherwise seems well and feeds normally.

If milk is spilling, your baby is gulping or repeatedly coughing, the flow may not be comfortable. Try the paced, nearly horizontal bottle position described by the NHS and ask your midwife or health visitor to watch a feed if it keeps happening. Do not assume that a faster teat, a different formula or a bigger bottle will solve distress.

If there is frequent spit-up alongside unsettled feeds, reflux is one possibility, not something a log can diagnose. The NHS reflux guidance recommends advice when a baby has feeding difficulty, keeps vomiting or seems uncomfortable. Our reflux and colic diary guide shows how to bring a short, useful record to that conversation without labelling every cry.

When to get help

Ask your midwife, health visitor or GP for feeding support if crying or difficulty feeding keeps recurring, your baby is taking much less milk than usual, has fewer wet nappies, is not gaining weight as expected, or seems unwell. A clinician can look at the baby and a whole feed; a feed total cannot do that.

Get urgent medical advice through your GP or NHS 111 if your baby is not interested in feeding or their cry is very different from usual. Call 999 if they are unresponsive, floppy, difficult to wake, struggling to breathe, or have blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin or lips, in line with NHS crying-baby guidance.

What is worth remembering for next time?

If you want to record it, keep it small: when the feed happened, roughly how much was actually taken, and one plain note such as “cried after a pause; coughed twice; settled when we stopped”.

Grandma Says My Baby Must Finish the Bottle. What Do I Say?

A calm, practical way to explain responsive bottle feeding to grandparents or carers, notice when baby is full, and hand over the amount actually taken.

Your baby turns away with milk still in the bottle. Grandma means well: “Just a little more, so they won't be hungry later.” You are grateful for the help, and you also want the feed to stop when your baby says it is done.

Your baby does not have to finish the bottle. The NHS advises carers to follow a baby's hunger and fullness cues, rather than a fixed schedule or an empty-bottle target. Babies often feed little and often. A larger bottle does not promise a longer gap before the next feed.

A sentence you can actually use

Try: “Thank you for feeding them. If they turn away or stop sucking, please pause and let them finish, even if some milk is left. Could you tell me roughly how much they drank and when?”

That gives your carer a clear action without making them feel they have failed. If they ask why, you can say: “The NHS says not to make babies finish a bottle. We're watching their cues, not aiming for a number.” You can send them the NHS feeding-on-demand guidance so the request does not rest on a debate between generations.

What does “finished” look like?

A baby may slow or stop sucking, turn away, push the teat out, spill milk, or relax and lose interest. A pause does not always mean the feed is over: let them rest, then offer again gently if they still seem interested. If they are done, stop. The NHS bottle-feeding advice says not to worry if a baby leaves milk, and never to force them to take more than they want.

For a comfortable feed, hold your baby close and semi-upright, keep the bottle almost horizontal so milk does not flow too fast, and stay with them throughout. Do not prop a bottle or leave a baby feeding alone; the NHS warns this can cause choking. If the worry is gulping rather than leaving milk, our paced-feeding guide is more relevant.

Make the handover about what happened, not a target

Before you leave, tell your carer how your baby usually shows hunger or needs a break, plus any feeding instructions a health professional has given your family. Afterward, ask for three simple details: when the feed began, roughly how much was taken, and whether your baby seemed comfortable. For example: “Offered 150 ml at 2 pm; took about 90 ml, then turned away and settled.” Prepared milk and milk actually drunk are different numbers.

If your baby has an individual feeding plan, concerns about weight gain, or instructions from a midwife, health visitor or clinician, share those with the carer and follow that advice. A general article cannot replace it. If you are unsure whether your baby is getting enough milk, ask your midwife or health visitor; the NHS looks at weight gain and wet nappies alongside feeds, not one unfinished bottle.

If your baby is not feeding, becomes unusually drowsy, or has fewer wet nappies than usual, seek prompt medical advice. The NHS dehydration guidance says to ask for an urgent GP appointment or contact NHS 111 for a baby with fewer wet nappies or unusual drowsiness. Do not solve those signs by urging a baby through the last few millilitres.

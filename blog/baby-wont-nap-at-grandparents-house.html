Baby Won't Nap at Grandparents' House? Try This Gentle Plan

A different house can make a baby nap feel difficult. Set up a safer sleep space, share two familiar cues and have a calm plan if the nap does not happen.

Grandma has made space for the travel cot. Your baby has had a cuddle, a feed and a tour of the living room. Then the nap starts—and suddenly they want no part of it. That does not mean the visit has failed or that anyone has forgotten how to settle them.

If your baby will not nap at the grandparents' house, check the sleep space first, keep one or two familiar settling cues, and let the carer respond to the baby in front of them. A short nap—or no nap on a first visit—is information, not a verdict on the grandparent or your routine. The NHS notes that babies have different sleep patterns and that a simple, soothing routine may help them settle. There is no guaranteed method or exact minute when an away-from-home nap must begin.

Before the visit: make the sleep place safe

Ask what cot or travel cot is available before you pack a sleep bag. The NHS safer-sleep guidance says to place babies on their backs in a clear cot or Moses basket with a firm, flat, waterproof mattress. For the first six months, their sleep space should be in the same room as the adult caring for them, for naps as well as nights.

Check the actual setup together: no pillows, nests, bumpers, toys or loose bedding near the baby's face. A travel cot's own thin mattress is not a problem if it is firm, flat and in good condition; do not add a folded blanket or an unapproved extra mattress to make it feel softer. The Lullaby Trust's away-from-home guidance explains why the manufacturer's instructions and a gap-free fit matter. If there is no suitable sleep place, agree another plan before the nap rather than improvising with a sofa, adult bed, bouncer or car seat indoors.

If a grandparent might doze during a cuddle, ask them to put baby into the safe sleep space first. Never fall asleep holding a baby on a sofa or armchair. Safety does not become optional because the visit is short.

Give the carer a two-minute nap brief

The useful handover is about today, not the baby's ideal schedule:

  • When did the last nap end, and when was the last feed?
  • Which two cues are familiar—a song, a sleep bag, a quiet cuddle or the usual phrase?
  • What does baby tend to do when they need a pause or more comfort?
  • Where is the safe sleep space, and who will be in the room if baby is under six months?
  • When should the grandparent call you instead of persisting?

For example: “She woke at 10:40 and fed at 11:15. We usually change her nappy, use this sleep bag and sing one verse. Try a cuddle if she protests; if she gets more upset, take a break and call me.” That gives a real person a next step, not a test to pass.

If this is their first solo nap together, a short practice visit while you are still there can make the room and routine familiar to both of them. Keep the plan flexible: the grandparent can offer comfort, pause, and try again when baby seems ready. The aim is responsive care, not reproducing your home nap to the minute.

If the nap does not happen

Ask for one clear update—“tried the cot, cuddled, still awake and content” is enough. If baby is calm, the carer can have a quiet break from the attempt and watch for tired cues. If baby is distressed, comfort them and check ordinary needs such as a feed or nappy. Avoid repeating a long, upsetting cot attempt solely to protect the schedule.

When baby comes home, ask what actually happened: whether they slept, for how long, and when the last feed was. Then make the evening gentler if they seem tired; do not assume an exact earlier bedtime will fix the day. Our guide to short naps is useful if brief naps happen in other places too. If your baby seems unwell, unusually difficult to wake or you are worried about feeding or breathing, seek medical advice rather than treating this as a routine nap problem.

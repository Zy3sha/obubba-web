When Can My Baby Use a Spoon? A Mess-Friendly Way to Practise

Your baby keeps grabbing the spoon, but little reaches their mouth. Here's when spoon skills usually emerge, a simple way to practise, and when to ask for help.

when can baby use a spoonbaby self feeding with spoonbaby grabbing spoon

Your baby grabs the spoon, turns it upside down and paints the tray with yoghurt. If very little reaches their mouth, are they too young to try?

No. Let them explore the spoon when they show interest, while you keep offering food in a way that works for them. There is no deadline to eat a whole meal with a utensil. The CDC says babies may begin using a spoon themselves at around 10 to 12 months, then get better with practice after their first birthday. That is a broad guide, not a test your baby must pass this week.

If they've just started solids

First check that your baby is ready for solids—usually around six months, with the ability to sit and hold their head steady, coordinate hand-to-mouth movements and swallow food. Our readiness guide explains the signs. If your baby was born prematurely or has a feeding or swallowing concern, ask your health visitor or clinical team for individual advice.

You can offer a spoon of suitable food and wait for your baby to open their mouth. If they reach for it, let them hold a second spoon or try a small amount on a spoon you have already loaded. A baby who only mouths the handle today is still exploring. The NHS advises letting babies touch and hold food and, if using a spoon, allowing them to hold that spoon or another one.

A two-spoon meal that does not need choreography

  1. Sit your baby upright in a securely harnessed highchair. Stay with them throughout the meal.
  2. Put a little age-appropriate, soft food on one spoon and offer it within reach. A thicker mash or plain yoghurt may stay on better than a runny purée; check ingredients and texture for your baby.
  3. Keep a second spoon for yourself if they want help. Offer it only when they open their mouth or lean in; let them pause or stop.
  4. If they turn the spoon over, drop it or use their fingers instead, that is fine. Try again another day. The aim is an opportunity to practise, not a spoonful quota.

The NHS says to follow your baby's hunger and fullness cues: wait for an open mouth, and stop if they firmly close it or turn away. You can be the grown-up holding the bowl without taking over every bite. A cloth under the chair is a perfectly respectable part of the plan.

What about finger food and the mess?

A spoon is one route into self-feeding, not the only one. Safe finger foods let your baby practise picking food up and bringing it to their mouth. The NHS suggests offering finger foods at each meal as they learn. Our baby-led versus spoon-feeding guide shows how the two can sit together, and our no-pressure guide to refused solids is there for days when food is simply not wanted.

Whatever the method, prepare food for your baby's current abilities, sit them upright and supervise closely. Avoid known choking shapes, including whole grapes and nuts; the NHS first-foods safety guidance explains how to prepare foods safely. Read our gagging-versus-choking guide and learn infant first aid before an emergency. Never put food into a baby's mouth to speed up a bite.

When to ask for support

If meals regularly involve coughing or choking, breathing changes, distress, difficulty swallowing, or worries about growth or how much your baby eats, speak to your health visitor or GP. Do not wait for a spoon milestone to decide whether a feeding concern deserves help. If your baby is choking or struggling to breathe now, call 999 and follow the NHS choking first-aid guidance. This article is general information, not an assessment of your child.

For an ordinary messy meal, you do not need to measure success in swallowed spoonfuls.

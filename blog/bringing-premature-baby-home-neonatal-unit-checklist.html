Bringing Your Premature Baby Home? What to Ask Before You Leave

A calm first-week checklist after neonatal care: the written feeding and medicine plan, follow-up contacts, safer sleep and a simple family handover.

premature baby home from neonatal unitneonatal discharge checklistfirst week home premature baby

The bag is packed. Your baby is coming home. It can feel wonderful and unsettling at the same time, especially when the people who knew every feed and alarm will not be in the next room.

Before leaving the neonatal unit, ask for one written plan you can actually use at home: feeding, medicines or equipment if needed, follow-up appointments, who to call in and out of hours, and the signs that mean you should seek help sooner. Your baby's own team and discharge letter take priority over a general checklist or an app. Chelsea and Westminster NHS describes giving families a discharge summary and arranging follow-up after neonatal care; the details depend on the baby.

This is general information for planning a conversation, not instructions for your baby's treatment. You do not have to memorise everything before you leave.

Five questions worth asking at the bedside

  1. What is today's feeding plan, in writing? Ask which feeds, amounts or supplements the team wants you to follow, what to record, and what change should prompt a call. If breastfeeding, expressing or a tube is part of your baby's plan, ask for a demonstration and a contact for feeding support. Do not replace an individual plan with an age-based schedule from the internet. Cambridge University Hospitals' neonatal outreach information describes home support for feeding plans, including specialist tube feeding where needed.
  2. Are there medicines, vitamins or equipment? Ask someone to show you each task, where the written instructions are, and how to get more supplies. If your baby is going home with a feeding tube or oxygen, clarify which adults have been trained to do what; do not pass a specialist task to an untrained helper through a text handover. The Cambridge NHS service describes parent sign-off for tube-feeding competencies before home care.
  3. Who is following up, and when? Put the neonatal, community, health visitor and clinic contacts in one place. Ask who to ring during the day, who to ring out of hours, and what to do if an appointment letter does not arrive. Chelsea and Westminster NHS describes discharge letters, health-visitor contact and follow-up for babies with complex needs; your local pathway may be different.
  4. What changes should make us call? Ask the team to write down the feeding, breathing, temperature or other signs that matter for your baby. If the answer sounds vague, it is reasonable to ask for a concrete example. The NHS urgent-help guide gives general emergency signs, including serious breathing difficulty, blue or grey colour, a baby who is hard to wake, or a baby you think is seriously unwell. In an emergency call 999; for uncertainty, use your team's contact or NHS 111.
  5. What should sleep look like at home? Hospital positioning or equipment can look different from a home cot. Ask the team to explain your home setup before discharge. The NHS safer-sleep guidance says the safest place for the first six months is a clear cot or Moses basket, on the back, in your room. It does not recommend co-sleeping if a baby was born premature or had a low birthweight. If you have been given a specific medical plan, clarify it with your neonatal team rather than improvising from what you saw on the unit.

Make the first handover smaller than the discharge letter

The discharge letter is the clinical record. A family handover is just a pointer to it plus the recent ordinary care details. A note on the fridge or in a shared message can be as short as:

  • Plan: Written neonatal feeding and medicine instructions are in [place]. Follow them; call [team number] if unclear.
  • Today: Last feed [time and detail the team asked us to track]; last nappy [time, if relevant]; next appointment [date].
  • Who is on: [adult] until [time]. For urgent concerns use [team's out-of-hours number]; for an emergency call 999.

Fill the blanks from your own paperwork. Do not put doses, tube steps or oxygen settings into a homemade template and assume a carer can safely act on them. Our general two-carer handover checklist can help with everyday details, but your neonatal plan always comes first.

Can Nursery Give My Baby a New Allergen?

Starting solids and nursery together? Agree a clear food plan: what your baby has tried, what is new, what ingredients are in meals and when to seek help.

introducing allergens at nurserybaby weaning nurserynursery food allergy plan

The nursery menu says fish pie. Your baby has tried potato and milk, but not fish. Is that fine? Should you ask nursery not to serve it? And what if you only find out at pickup?

There is no universal rule that a baby's first taste of an allergen must happen at home. What matters is that the adults feeding your baby agree which foods are new, know the ingredients, introduce potential allergens one at a time in small amounts, and can recognise and respond to a reaction. Many families find it simpler to plan a first taste for a time when they can see what was eaten and observe their baby; that is a practical arrangement, not an NHS requirement to ban new foods at nursery. If your baby has a diagnosed allergy, eczema or an individual clinical plan, discuss introductions with your GP or health visitor first.

Agree the plan before the first meal

Ask your nursery or childminder how they handle new foods, ingredient lists, allergies and mealtime records. Then give them three short lists:

  • Tried and usually eaten: “Well-cooked omelette; plain yoghurt; wheat toast.” Say how it was served if that matters.
  • Not yet tried or not confirmed: “We have not introduced fish or sesame. Please tell us before a meal containing either.”
  • Reaction or medical plan: “A reaction was suspected after egg; we are waiting for clinical advice.” Attach the setting's required written care plan, not an app screenshot alone.

“Tried” does not mean “guaranteed safe forever.” It only records what your baby ate and what you observed. If the nursery provides food, request the actual ingredients for mixed dishes, sauces and snacks; the dish name alone may hide milk, wheat, egg, fish or sesame. The Food Standards Agency's current allergen guidance for food businesses stresses knowing the ingredients in each dish and keeping that information up to date when recipes change. Each setting has its own food and care procedures, so agree how menu changes will be communicated.

What does official weaning guidance say?

The NHS advises introducing foods that can trigger allergy from around six months, one at a time and in very small amounts so a reaction is easier to spot. Once a food is introduced and tolerated, keep offering it as part of the baby's usual diet. A nursery meal can be one of those regular occasions—but a food log does not diagnose tolerance or replace an allergy plan.

If your baby has eczema, a diagnosed food allergy or a relevant family allergy history, the NHS says to speak with a GP or health visitor before introducing foods. Do not use a nursery menu or app prompt to override individual advice. Equally, a blanket decision to postpone all common allergens indefinitely is not what the NHS recommends for most babies.

A useful question for Monday morning

“We're introducing foods one at a time. Could you tell us if today's meals contain something Maya hasn't tried yet? We can agree whether she has that dish today or an alternative, and please note what she actually ate.”

That keeps the conversation collaborative. It also leaves room for the nursery's own policies and for a baby who eats only a tiny amount. You do not need a perfect spreadsheet; you need an honest record of what was offered and what was swallowed, if known.

If a mixed meal has already happened, ask for the recipe or ingredient list before deciding what was introduced. “Fish pie” might include fish, milk and wheat, but recipes vary. Do not tick every possible allergen from the name alone. Our guide to tracking allergens in mixed baby meals shows how to record uncertainty without turning it into a false fact.

Keep texture and supervision in the same conversation

Allergen lists are only one part of safe eating. Tell the setting which textures your baby currently manages, whether they use a spoon or finger foods, and what preparation has worked. The NHS advises sitting babies safely upright and staying with them while they eat. Food shape and texture matter; a familiar ingredient served differently can still be hard for a baby to manage. Ask who will supervise meals and how they will tell you what was eaten, rather than assuming the daily menu equals the baby's actual intake.

Where OBubba can help—and where it cannot

In the current app, Track → Feed → Solids records a food and an optional parent-observed response. Care → Weaning → Progress → Food journal brings those entries together, while Care → Weaning → Learn → Allergen journey shows groups recognised from logged foods or marked manually. Before a nursery handover, check the actual entries and send a short, corrected list of foods eaten, not yet confirmed and any concern. A food entered in the log may appear as “introduced” even when the baby only tasted or rejected it. OBubba may recognise an allergen from the words you enter, but it cannot inspect a nursery recipe, detect hidden ingredients, confirm what your baby swallowed or certify that a food is safe.

If nursery reports a suspected reaction, get the details—food, ingredients, amount, timing and what they saw—and follow the baby's care plan. Seek clinical advice before offering that food again. The NHS says a severe reaction with breathing difficulty or throat or tongue swelling is an emergency; call 999 in the UK. Log the event later, once the baby is safe. For a gentle way to track first tastes without over-recording, see how to introduce baby allergens without losing track.

When the nursery day ends, one useful pickup question is enough: “What did they actually eat, and was anything new?” Download OBubba if one calm food record would make that answer easier to keep across home and nursery.

This UK-oriented article is general information, not medical advice. Follow your child's individual allergy, feeding and nursery care plan; ask a GP, health visitor or allergy team when you are unsure, and seek urgent or emergency help for concerning symptoms. OBubba is a parent-entered record, not an allergy test or a substitute for ingredient information.

In the US or UK? If you are worried, speak to your pediatrician, health visitor or GP. For urgent help, call 911 in the US, or 999 or NHS 111 in the UK.

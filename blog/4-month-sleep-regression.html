4 Month Sleep Regression: Signs, How Long It Lasts and What Helps

The 4 month sleep regression explained calmly: why sleep changes around 3 to 5 months, common signs, how long it tends to last and gentle things that help, with sources.

The short answer: the "4 month sleep regression" is the popular name for a change in sleep that many babies go through somewhere around 3 to 5 months. Naps can get shorter and night waking can increase for a while. There is no study that pins down exactly how long it lasts, and many families find the most unsettled stretch passes within a few weeks as their baby adjusts. Every baby is different, and some barely show it at all.

If it feels as though your baby's sleep went backwards overnight, you are not doing anything wrong. Here is what is known, what is not, and what tends to help.

Is the 4 month sleep regression real?

"Sleep regression" is a phrase parents and sleep writers use. It is not a medical diagnosis, and you will not find it on NHS pages. Cleveland Clinic's paediatric sleep article puts it well: research has not shown regressions "happening like clockwork at a specific age for every baby" (Cleveland Clinic).

What is well documented is that a baby's sleep changes a lot in the first months:

  • The American Academy of Pediatrics says "babies do not have regular sleep cycles until about 4 months of age" (HealthyChildren.org, AAP).
  • Sleep scientists describe deeper slow wave sleep first appearing at around 2 to 3 months after the due date and usually being present by about 4 to 4.5 months (Grigg-Damberger and colleagues, 2007).
  • A study that followed babies across their first year found the fastest consolidation of sleep happened in the first 4 months (Henderson and colleagues, 2010).

So rather than a step backwards, it is better thought of as sleep growing up. Your baby now moves through clearer cycles of lighter and deeper sleep. In one 2026 study that tracked movement during sleep, infant sleep cycles averaged about an hour, shorter than their parents' (Hammad and colleagues, 2026). Between cycles there are lighter moments, and some babies now notice them and need help to drift back off.

Signs of the 4 month sleep regression

These are common, not a checklist your baby has to match:

  • Naps that suddenly end after a short stretch, often around the length of one sleep cycle
  • More night wakes than a few weeks ago
  • Settling at bedtime taking longer than it used to
  • Waking more easily when put down, or soon after
  • Busier, more alert days, sometimes alongside rolling practice or extra interest in feeds

The NHS reminds parents that "all babies change their sleep patterns" and that your baby "will have their own pattern of waking and sleeping, and it's unlikely to be the same as other babies you know" (NHS: Helping your baby to sleep).

How long does the 4 month sleep regression last?

Nobody has measured this well, so be wary of exact numbers. Cleveland Clinic says regressions "typically last a week or two" (Cleveland Clinic), and many families describe a few unsettled weeks. The new way of sleeping is here to stay; what usually eases is the disruption, as your baby gets used to it.

It also helps to know that night waking is normal at this age and well beyond. The NHS says that at 3 to 6 months "some babies may sleep for 5 to 8 hours or longer at night, but not all" (NHS). A study of 388 Canadian families found that 27.9% to 57.0% of babies at 6 and 12 months did not sleep through the night, depending on the definition used, and that this was not linked to their development or to their mothers' mood (Pennestri and colleagues, 2018).

What helps (the gentle version)

You do not need to sleep train your way through this. A few calm, consistent things tend to make the biggest difference:

  • Watch your baby's cues and recent awake time. Typical ranges are a rough guide, not a timetable. Our wake windows by age table shows the usual ranges.
  • Keep a short, predictable wind-down. The same few steps each time (dim lights, nappy, sleeping bag, a quiet feed or song) help your baby know what comes next.
  • Keep the sleep space dark, calm and safe. The Lullaby Trust advises always putting your baby to sleep on their back in a clear cot or sleep space, in the same room as you for at least the first six months, day and night (The Lullaby Trust). That still applies on the hardest nights.
  • Give a moment to resettle. When your baby stirs, a short pause can show whether they are drifting back off. Respond whenever they need you.
  • Protect daytime sleep and full feeds. A day with enough sleep and milk often makes the evening easier.
  • Look after yourself. Share the nights if you can, lower the bar on everything else, and remember this stage passes.

When it might be something else

A few unsettled weeks around this age are common. If sleep is getting steadily harder with no sign of settling, other things can play a part too, such as illness, teething, a cold, or a routine that has drifted. If you are worried about feeding, weight, breathing or how your baby seems in general, talk to your health visitor or GP, or call NHS 111. Trust your instincts.

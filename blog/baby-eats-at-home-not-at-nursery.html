My Baby Eats at Home but Not at Nursery. What Helps?

If your baby leaves nursery meals untouched but eats at home, try a calm handover, familiar food and a whole-day view without pressuring them to finish.

baby not eating at nurserybaby eats at home not nurserynursery food refusal

The nursery note says “hardly touched lunch”. Then your baby comes home and happily eats with you. It is unsettling, but one small nursery meal does not tell you how much your baby ate across the whole day, or why they declined that meal. Start by asking what was offered, when, and how the meal went. Keep feeding familiar and unpressured at home while you and the carers look for a pattern.

If your baby is only just starting solids, small amounts are expected. The NHS says early meals are about learning tastes and textures, while breast milk or first infant formula still provides most energy and nutrients. As babies grow, food matters increasingly, so repeated refusal deserves a conversation rather than a shrug.

Ask for the story, not just “ate none”

At pick-up, one kind question can be more useful than a percentage: “What happened when lunch came out?” You might learn that your baby was tired, the meal came close to a milk feed, the texture was unfamiliar, or they wanted to hold the spoon themselves. Those are possibilities to explore, not a diagnosis.

Ask the carers to note just three things for a few days:

  1. What and when: the food offered, its texture and roughly when it was served.
  2. What your baby did: reached, tasted, turned away, closed their mouth, played, coughed or seemed uncomfortable.
  3. What happened next: whether they had their usual milk feed and how they seemed later.

This is a handover, not a performance report. Avoid turning every morsel into a score for the carer or the baby. Our two-carer handover guide has a simple way to agree what information is genuinely useful.

Make one small change at a time

If nursery allows it, agree on a familiar, age-appropriate food or texture your baby already manages at home. Ask whether the meal can happen when they are alert and not rushed. If they are learning to self-feed, a spoon to hold or a suitable finger food may help them take part. The NHS encourages letting babies go at their own pace and stopping when they show they have had enough. Do not ask carers to keep offering a spoon after your baby turns away or closes their mouth.

Keep agreed milk feeds in the plan. Breast milk or first infant formula remains the main drink throughout the first year; a missed nursery lunch is not a reason to replace milk with a different drink or to force a larger dinner. If you are unsure how milk and solids fit together at this stage, see our milk-after-starting-solids guide.

Agree on allergies and food safety before trying anything new. Nursery should know which foods your baby has already had, any diagnosed allergy or care plan, and who to contact if there is a reaction. Our guide to new allergens at nursery explains why this should be a shared decision, not a surprise at pick-up. Food should always be prepared in an age-appropriate way and your baby supervised while eating.

Look at several days, not one lunch

At home, you can keep the record tiny: nursery offered pasta and peas; baby tasted peas, left pasta; had usual milk; ate some dinner. In OBubba, Track → Feed → Solids lets you record the food and a simple response such as “Loved it” or “Unsure”. It can help two carers remember what happened, but it cannot judge whether your baby has eaten enough or explain a refusal. You do not need to log every bite.

If several nursery meals are refused, ask how your baby is eating and drinking across the entire day, and whether the pattern also appears at home. For a baby who is otherwise well, the next step may be a calmer meal setup or a conversation with the key person. If the refusal persists, your baby seems uncomfortable with eating, or you are worried about growth or nutrition, speak to your health visitor or GP. Our no-pressure solids plan offers more ideas for making mealtimes manageable.

Ask for an urgent GP appointment or call NHS 111 if your baby has fewer wet nappies than usual, is feeding much less overall, or seems unwell. Call 999 or go to A&E if they have breathing difficulty, are difficult to wake, or have other signs of serious illness, in line with NHS urgent-help guidance.

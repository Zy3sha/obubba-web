Baby Rolls One Way but Not the Other: Should I Worry?

If your baby rolls tummy to back but not back to tummy, or always rolls towards one side, here's what to notice, how to offer gentle play and when to ask for help.

baby rolling one waybaby rolling milestonesbaby movement concerns

Your baby rolled from their tummy to their back. You cheered, then waited for the return trip. A week later, they are still going only one way. Or perhaps they roll to the right but never to the left. Are those the same thing?

Not quite. Tummy-to-back and back-to-tummy are different skills, and babies often learn them at different times. A consistent preference for one side of the body, though, deserves a closer look—particularly if your baby also turns their head only one way or moves one arm or leg less. You do not need to work out the cause yourself. If it persists or worries you, ask your health visitor or GP.

First, which "one way" do you mean?

  • Tummy to back, but not back to tummy: These two directions can arrive separately. The NHS describes tummy-to-back as commonly coming first. Notice your baby's other movement too, rather than treating the missing second roll as a deadline.
  • Always over the same shoulder: Try noticing whether your baby turns their head, reaches and kicks comfortably on both sides during awake play. One roll to a favourite side is not a diagnosis. But West Yorkshire's NHS-backed movement guidance says to seek advice if one side of the body, arms or legs moves differently or seems weaker than the other. Speak with a health visitor or GP if you keep seeing that pattern.
  • A roll they could do has disappeared: Do not wait for another milestone chart. The same NHS-backed guidance says to seek help urgently if a child loses skills they had gained. Tell a healthcare professional what changed and when.

Age guides are starting points, not pass-or-fail tests. The CDC's six-month milestone page lists tummy-to-back rolling among skills most babies show by that age and recommends speaking with a doctor when you have a concern. If your baby was born early, ask your clinician how to use corrected age when looking at development.

What can I do during awake play?

Think opportunity, not practice until they perform. A few calm minutes count.

  1. Use a clear, safe floor space. Sit close during awake, supervised tummy-time or back play. A firm mat on the floor gives room to move without the fall risk of a sofa or changing table. If tummy-time is difficult, try short, comfortable moments and follow your baby's cues. Our tummy-time guide has low-pressure ways to begin.
  2. Invite a look and reach. Put a suitable toy within view, just to one side, then later offer it on the other side. The CDC recommends toys just out of reach during supervised floor play; NHS rolling guidance also suggests offering toys to encourage movement. Let your baby choose whether to reach, turn or rest.
  3. Notice the whole picture. Are they looking both ways? Using both arms and legs? Is the preference occasional or consistent? A short note is more useful for a professional than trying to recreate a perfect video of a roll. If your baby becomes upset, pause the game.

There is no need to push a shoulder, pull a limb or make a baby complete a roll. If you are worried about movement, ask a health visitor or GP for advice tailored to your child rather than trying a technique from a video.

When should I ask for help?

You can ask at any age if something feels different or you are uneasy. It is especially worth contacting your health visitor or GP if your baby consistently favours one side, has difficulty turning their head, seems unusually stiff or floppy while awake, uses one arm or leg much less, is not attempting to roll as expected, or has lost a skill. The NHS advice on head-turning and head shape also recommends getting advice if a baby's head is always tilted to one side or turning is difficult. A professional can look at your baby as a whole; a milestone list cannot.

If you call, say what you have seen in plain terms: "She rolled from tummy to back twice this week, always over her right shoulder. She looks left for toys but seems reluctant to turn her body that way." The details matter more than a neat milestone date.

Does rolling change sleep safety?

Always place your baby on their back to start every sleep. Keep the sleep space clear, and stop swaddling when your baby shows signs of trying to roll. Do not use pillows, wedges or positioners to keep them on one side or stop a roll. The AAP's safe-sleep guidance explains that once a baby can roll comfortably both ways, they can generally be left in a position they reach themselves in an otherwise safe sleep space. If yours can roll only one way, speak to your health visitor or GP about any sleep-position concern instead of improvising a restraint.

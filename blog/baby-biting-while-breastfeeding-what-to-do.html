Baby Bit Me While Breastfeeding. What Do I Do at the Next Feed?

A calm next-feed plan when your baby bites during breastfeeding: release safely, notice when it happens, protect a sore nipple and know when to ask for help.

baby biting while breastfeedingbaby bit nipple breastfeedingbreastfeeding after baby bites

One sharp bite can make the next feed feel frightening. You may find yourself watching your baby's mouth instead of relaxing into the cuddle. That reaction makes sense; it hurt.

If your baby bites, gently break the suction at the corner of their mouth before taking them off the breast. Pause, then try again if you both want to. A bite does not automatically mean breastfeeding has to end. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends breaking suction before removing the breast when sucking stops. You are allowed to stop a feed that hurts and ask for support.

In the moment: release, pause, reset

If you notice your baby stop actively drinking and clamp down, avoid pulling them straight off while they are still attached. Slip a clean little finger into the corner of their mouth to break the suction, then take them off gently. Take a breath. You can offer the breast again when you feel ready, or take a short break.

Keep your response simple. You do not need to shout, punish or keep feeding through pain. If your baby is no longer interested in milk, ending that feed may be enough. The Australian Breastfeeding Association suggests calmly taking a baby off the breast after a bite and watching for the pause in active feeding that can come before one.

At the next feed, notice when it happens

There is no single cause you can diagnose from one bite. A baby might clamp down as a feed winds down or when their attention wanders; sometimes a baby seems impatient before milk begins to flow. Teeth do not mean every feed will hurt.

Try one small adjustment, not an elaborate new routine:

  • Offer the feed when your baby is showing interest, and find a calmer spot if distractions seem to be part of the moment.
  • Notice active sucking and swallowing. When that stops and your baby is playing at the breast, gently unlatch and see whether they want a pause.
  • If your baby wants to chew before a feed, offer an age-appropriate teether instead of letting your nipple do that job.

These are options from Australian Breastfeeding Association guidance, not a promise that the next feed will be bite-free. If biting keeps happening, ask a breastfeeding supporter or health visitor to watch a feed. They can help you look at attachment and what is happening for your baby.

If your nipple is sore or broken

Check the skin after the feed. If it is cracked or bleeding, seek help early rather than trying to tolerate repeated injury. The NHS advises contacting a midwife, health visitor or breastfeeding supporter if nipples crack or bleed, or pain happens at every feed. They can observe a feed and help with positioning and attachment. Ask a GP or NHS 111 for medical advice if you feel unwell or have breast or nipple pain that does not go away; NHS guidance notes that pain can sometimes involve infection.

If your baby starts taking much less milk or you notice fewer wet nappies, seek feeding advice promptly rather than relying on a feeding timer to reassure you. The NHS explains the wider signs of enough milk; time at the breast cannot measure how much milk was taken. This is general information, not a way to assess an individual baby's intake.

Let the record do the remembering, not the diagnosing

My Six-Month-Old Can't Sit Alone Yet. Should I Worry?

Independent sitting is not the six-month milestone. Here is what to notice, how to give your baby safe floor time, and when to ask for help.

baby not sitting at six monthswhen do babies sit independentlysix month sitting milestone

You sit your baby on the mat, move your hands away, and they fold sideways in two seconds. Then you see another baby the same age sitting up for a photo. It is hard not to wonder if you have missed something.

Not sitting alone at six months is not, by itself, a missed six-month milestone. The CDC's six-month guide describes a baby leaning on their hands while sitting. Its nine-month guide lists sitting without support and getting into a sitting position. Those ages are guides to what most babies do, not a deadline or a diagnosis. If something about your baby's movement worries you, you can ask your health visitor now; you do not need to wait for a birthday.

Three different things people mean by “sitting”

  • Supported sitting: you hold your baby securely while they look around. They are not expected to balance alone.
  • Leaning on hands: your baby sits briefly with their own hands forward on the floor for support. This is the movement in the CDC's six-month list.
  • Sitting independently: your baby balances without your hands or their hands propping them up. The CDC lists this by nine months.

A photo rarely shows which stage came before it—or the adult hand just outside the frame. Look at your baby's movement across ordinary days, not a one-second comparison.

What can I do today?

Give your baby a little supervised time on a firm floor mat, on their tummy or back, with a toy close enough to invite looking and reaching. Sit with them. When they seem interested, hold them securely in a supported sitting position for a short look around, then return to floor play or a rest when they are done. The CDC suggests floor play and supported sitting; it does not require a timed training programme.

Stay within arm's reach when your baby is sitting because a wobble can become a fall quickly. Do not leave them propped up with cushions or a feeding pillow, especially on a sofa, bed or other raised surface. Play is an opportunity to explore movement, not a test they must pass today.

You might notice whether your baby can hold their head steady when you support them, reach for a toy, push up during tummy time, or use both sides of their body. You do not need to turn every play session into an assessment. If you are concerned about a particular movement, make a short note of what you saw and ask a professional to look with you.

Does not sitting alone mean we cannot start solids?

Independent sitting and safe positioning for a meal are not the same question. The NHS first-foods guide says readiness signs appear together from around six months: your baby can stay in a sitting position and hold their head steady, coordinate eyes, hands and mouth to bring food to their mouth, and swallow rather than push food straight back out. They need to sit safely upright in a high chair with its harness secured, under close supervision. An NHS health-visiting service clarifies that a baby may still need the chair's support and need not sit independently.

If your baby cannot keep their head steady or remain safely upright even with appropriate support, pause solids and ask your health visitor or GP about readiness. If your baby was born prematurely, ask for individual advice rather than using calendar age alone. The NHS weaning guide specifically recommends this for premature babies.

When should I ask for help about sitting?

Speak to your health visitor or GP if your baby seems unusually floppy or stiff, uses one side much more than the other, has lost a skill they had, or you are simply worried about how they move. If they are not leaning on their hands to support sitting around six months, or are not sitting without support around nine months, bring that observation up rather than waiting to see whether an app changes its label. The CDC advises sharing missing milestones, lost skills and any concern with a clinician. In the UK, a health visitor or GP can consider the whole picture and whether an assessment is needed.

If your baby was born early, tell the clinician their due date as well as their birth date. Our corrected-age guide explains why that context matters. A sudden loss of movement or an acutely unwell baby needs prompt medical advice, not a milestone checklist.

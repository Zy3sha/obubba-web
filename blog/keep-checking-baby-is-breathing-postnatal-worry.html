I Keep Checking My Baby Is Breathing: When Should I Ask for Help?

A gentle guide for the parent who keeps checking their sleeping baby: when checking becomes a worry loop, how to describe it to a professional and where to get urgent help.

checking baby breathingpostnatal anxietynew parent worry

You look at your sleeping baby. Their breathing seems fine. You go back to bed, then feel an urge to check again. A few minutes later, you are standing beside them once more. If that is your night, you are not a bad parent and you do not have to untangle it alone.

A check because something has changed is different from a check that only eases a frightening thought for a moment. If checking is repeated, hard to resist, stealing sleep or making everyday care difficult, tell your health visitor or GP. You do not need to decide whether it is “normal worry”, anxiety or OCD before you ask. If your baby actually appears unwell or is not breathing normally, that is a separate medical emergency: call 999.

How can I tell when checking has become a worry loop?

Look at what happens after the check. Do you feel reassured long enough to rest, or does the same thought return almost immediately? Do you feel you must check in exactly the same way again and again? Are you losing sleep even when someone else is caring for the baby?

The NHS describes repeated checking of a baby's breathing as a possible compulsion in postnatal OCD. That does not mean every parent who looks in on a baby has OCD. It means that persistent, distressing checking is a real reason to seek help, not a test of how devoted you are. NHS guidance recommends speaking to a GP or health visitor when unwanted thoughts and repeated behaviours affect daily life.

Frightening “what if” thoughts can also arrive uninvited. Birmingham and Solihull Mental Health NHS Foundation Trust explains that intrusive thoughts are unwanted, and having a thought is not the same as doing something. If thoughts are frequent, intensely upsetting or interfering with caring for your baby, tell a professional promptly. You do not need to hide the words because they feel embarrassing.

What can I do tonight without turning this into another rule?

  1. Treat a real concern as a real concern. If your baby is unresponsive or not breathing normally, the NHS says to call 999 and start baby CPR straight away; follow the call handler's instructions. If your baby has an individual medical monitoring plan, follow that plan and ask their team about any uncertainty.
  2. Let one evidence-based sleep setup do its job. Put your baby down on their back in a clear, safe sleep space, following current NHS safe-sleep advice. You can read our side-sleeping and rolling guide for that separate question. A baby tracker, camera or timer cannot certify that a baby is safe.
  3. Tell another adult what is happening. Try: “I know I've checked and they look okay, but I'm being pulled back to check again. I'm losing sleep. Can you sit with me while I contact the health visitor tomorrow?” If a trusted adult can take an agreed care shift, let it be an actual handover, not another job you supervise from your phone.

This is not a do-it-yourself programme to stop checking. A clinician can help you work out what kind of support fits your situation. If you are also exhausted but cannot sleep during a genuine off-duty period, our postpartum insomnia guide explains why that deserves attention too.

What should I say when I ask for help?

You can be wonderfully direct: “Since the baby arrived, I check their breathing many times each night. I know they look okay, but the fear comes straight back. It's affecting my sleep and I want help.” Mention how often it happens, when it began, whether you avoid being away from your baby, and any upsetting thoughts you are having. A GP, midwife or health visitor can listen, assess what is going on and help you access support. The NHS lists talking therapies and GP care as routes for OCD symptoms; you do not need an app score or a diagnosis before starting that conversation.

When is the help urgent?

If you think you might act on thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, or you cannot keep either of you safe, call 999 or go to A&E now. For urgent mental-health help that is not an immediate emergency in England, call NHS 111 and select the mental-health option, or ask for an urgent GP appointment. If you are suddenly confused, hearing or seeing things others do not, or developing unusual beliefs—especially soon after birth—the NHS says possible postpartum psychosis needs urgent same-day assessment. A trusted adult can help make the call and stay with you.

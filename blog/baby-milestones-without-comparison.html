Baby Milestones Without the Comparison Spiral

A calm way to notice your baby’s development, keep a useful milestone record and know when to ask a health visitor or GP for help.

A milestone list is most useful as a conversation starter and memory prompt, not a scorecard. Notice what your baby is beginning to do across movement, communication, play and relationships; write down only the moments you want to remember or discuss; and ask for help when you are concerned rather than waiting for an app to decide.

Babies do not complete development in a neat order. One may concentrate on moving while another is busy experimenting with sounds. Variation is expected. At the same time, reassurance should never become “wait and see” when your instinct says something has changed.

What a milestone checklist can—and cannot—tell you

Developmental monitoring means noticing how a child changes over time: how they move, play, learn, communicate and relate to people. A short checklist can help you remember examples and prepare for a conversation.

It cannot diagnose a developmental delay. The CDC explains that its milestone checklists are not screening or diagnostic tools. Its listed milestones are placed at ages when at least 75% of children would be expected to show them, specifically to help concerns lead to a proper conversation, screening or evaluation—not to grade an individual child.

In the UK, the NHS offers health and development reviews and encourages families to keep the developmental section of the red book up to date. The NHS baby review guide says you can contact your health visitor, GP or baby clinic whenever you have concerns; you do not need to wait for the 9-to-12-month or 2-to-2-and-a-half-year review.

A low-pressure way to notice development

Instead of running through a long list every day, use four gentle questions once in a while.

1. What is new?

Look for a small change, not a polished performance. Perhaps your baby now:

  • turns towards a familiar voice;
  • reaches more deliberately;
  • makes a new sound;
  • looks for an object that disappeared;
  • uses a gesture to ask for something; or
  • joins a familiar game in a new way.

You do not have to recreate the moment on demand. A skill seen naturally in ordinary life is still a skill.

2. What are they practising?

Development often looks unfinished before it looks obvious. A baby may rock before crawling, pull up without knowing how to get down, or make repeated sounds before a recognisable word appears.

Write “beginning to…” if that is what you saw. This keeps the record honest and stops an emerging skill being forced into a yes-or-no box.

3. Is the change showing up in more than one setting?

A child may behave differently when tired, hungry, unwell, overwhelmed or with an unfamiliar person. If a question matters, notice it across a few ordinary moments rather than staging a test.

Another carer can add useful context: “She points to the cup at nursery too” is more helpful than “she passed pointing”. A short baby care handover can carry that observation without turning grandparents or nursery into assessors.

4. Is anything worrying you?

The useful question is not only “is this box ticked?” It is also:

  • Has my baby lost a skill they previously used?
  • Are they using one side of their body very differently from the other?
  • Am I worried about hearing, vision, movement, communication or interaction?
  • Does feeding, breathing, pain or illness seem to be affecting what they can do?
  • Is my concern persistent even if other people are reassuring me?

The CDC milestone guidance says to act early if a child is not meeting one or more milestones, has lost skills or you have another concern. For a UK family, that means speaking to your health visitor or GP and asking whether developmental screening or another assessment is appropriate. A record can support that conversation, but it should not delay it.

What is actually worth recording?

A useful milestone note can be one sentence:

  • What happened: “Reached for the spoon and brought it to her mouth.”
  • When: an exact date, month or simply “this week”.
  • Context, if useful: “At breakfast, sitting with support.”
  • Question: “Only seems to reach with the right hand—worth discussing?”

Photos and videos can be lovely memories, but they are optional. Do not repeatedly film your baby trying to capture proof. If you want to show a specific movement, sound or interaction to a professional, a brief natural example may help—provided your child is safe and comfortable.

There is usually no need to record:

  • every attempt;
  • a daily milestone total;
  • comparisons with another baby;
  • a predicted achievement date as a promise;
  • “advanced” or “behind” as a permanent label; or
  • a skill you cannot remember seeing just to complete a list.

If your baby was born preterm, use the right age context

For children born preterm, developmental expectations may use corrected age, calculated from the original due date. NICE recommends corrected age for the first 2 years when assessing functional and developmental skills, while also making clear that parents should raise concerns at any stage.

That context is not a reason to dismiss a concern. It is a way to compare with a more appropriate developmental age. Your neonatal team, paediatrician, health visitor or GP can explain which follow-up applies to your child.

A five-minute milestone check-in

Try this monthly, before a health review, or whenever something changes:

  1. Write down one new thing your baby does.
  2. Note one skill they seem to be practising.
  3. Add one thing they enjoy doing with you.
  4. Write one question, even if it feels small.
  5. Stop there.

This produces a clearer picture than opening a checklist every day. It also leaves room for the part worth celebrating: your baby is learning through ordinary moments, not performing for a chart.

Quick questions

My friend’s baby is doing something mine is not. Should I worry?

One comparison cannot answer that. Look at your child’s broader development and the age-appropriate guidance, then speak to a health visitor or GP if a milestone is missing or anything concerns you.

Should I wait until the next health visitor review?

No. NHS guidance says you can contact your health visitor or GP at other times. Raise a concern when you have it, especially if your child has lost a skill.

Is a milestone tracker a developmental screening tool?

No. A tracker helps with developmental monitoring and memory. Screening uses validated tools with a trained professional; assessment and diagnosis require appropriate clinicians.

Do I need the exact date for every first?

No. “Around nine months” or “this week” may be enough. Use an exact date when it is meaningful to your family or useful for a professional conversation.

This article provides general information, not medical advice or developmental screening. If you are worried about your child’s development, speak to your health visitor or GP. Seek urgent medical help for an acute emergency.

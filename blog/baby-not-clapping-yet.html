My Baby Isn't Clapping Yet. Should I Worry?

Not clapping at 12 months does not answer everything about development. Here is what to notice, how to play without pressure and when to speak to a health visitor.

baby not clapping at 12 monthswhen do babies clapbaby clapping milestone

You clap at the end of a song. Your baby grins, bangs the table and wants it again—but does not put their hands together. If your baby is not clapping at 12 months, that one observation cannot tell you whether there is a developmental problem. Look at how they play and communicate more broadly. You can also ask your health visitor or GP now if you are worried; you do not have to wait for a particular birthday.

The CDC lists “claps when excited” among milestones most children do by 15 months. “Most” means at least 75%, not every child, and a milestone list is not a diagnostic test. A baby who does not clap by 15 months, has lost a skill, or worries you for any other reason deserves a conversation with a health professional rather than a wait-and-see rule made from one tick box.

Notice the interaction, not just the hand movement

Clapping takes coordination, but it is also one way of joining in. During an ordinary song or game, you might notice whether your baby:

  • looks at you or turns towards your voice
  • smiles, vocalises or moves when something delights them
  • copies another simple action, such as tapping a table
  • takes a turn in a familiar game
  • shows you something interesting or reaches to share a moment

These are things to describe, not a home assessment or a replacement checklist. Babies show interest in different ways. If you notice little interaction as well as no clapping, mention the whole picture to your health visitor or GP. Our first-year milestones guide can help you think beyond a single skill.

A small, no-pressure way to play

Try one short song your baby already likes. Clap your own hands once or twice, pause and watch what they do. If they bounce, tap, smile or make a sound, respond to that. You can repeat the game later, but there is no need to run a daily clapping test or make your baby copy you.

The NHS suggests singing, talking and clapping together as ways to play. If your baby enjoys hand games, you can gently clap together; stop if they pull away or are not interested. A wooden spoon on a pan, a wave or a little dance can make the same moment of shared fun without requiring a perfect clap.

When should I ask for advice?

Ask your health visitor or GP whenever you are concerned, including before 15 months. Tell them what you see in everyday play, not only “my baby isn't clapping”. The NHS says families can contact a health visitor or GP between routine development reviews. The 9-to-12-month review is another chance to bring questions, and you can take your baby's red book.

If your baby has lost a skill they previously used, seek professional advice promptly rather than waiting for the next review. The CDC advises acting early if a child loses skills, misses milestones or you have other concerns. Our guide to a lost skill explains what to note before that conversation. If your baby was born prematurely, tell the professional; the NHS explains that developmental age is calculated from the due date until age two.

You could say: “At songs, they smile and tap the table, but I haven't seen a clap. They do / don't copy other actions. Is there anything you would like to check?” A short, specific description is more useful than trying to decide at home whether your baby has “passed”.

My Baby Isn't Walking at 12 Months. Should I Worry?

Not walking at 12 months can be normal. See which movement signs matter, how to make space for practice, and when to ask a health visitor for help.

The birthday photos are done, and someone has already asked, “Is your baby walking yet?” It is a small question that can sit heavily when the answer is no.

Not walking independently at 12 months, on its own, is not a reason to panic. The CDC's one-year movement checklist includes pulling up to stand and walking while holding furniture—not taking unaided steps. Look at how your baby is moving overall, and ask your health visitor if progress worries you. You never have to wait for a birthday or a checklist cut-off to ask.

Look for the steps before the first step

Can your baby get around in their own way? Are they trying to pull up, taking weight through their feet, or moving sideways while holding furniture (often called cruising)? These details tell a health visitor more than “not walking yet”. The Oxford Health NHS children's therapy guidance describes moving into standing and cruising as useful parts of learning to walk.

You do not need to test your baby or compare them with another child. Notice what they do naturally during play. “Pulls up at the sofa and cruises both ways, but hasn't stepped away from it” is a useful description. So is “doesn't seem to try to stand or put weight on their feet”.

Milestone lists are a way to start a conversation, not a pass-or-fail exam. The CDC puts a few independent steps on its 15-month checklist and walking without holding on on its 18-month checklist. Those ages do not tell you exactly when your child will walk.

Give them room to move, without drilling walking

A little supervised floor play in a safe space gives your baby chances to shift, reach, crawl or move in another way, pull up and try again. If they are starting to stand, check that furniture is stable and that sharp corners, stairs and loose items are made safe. Stay close. You can play nearby and let them choose the movement; there is no need to pull them along by their hands or turn every playtime into a walking lesson.

You do not need to buy a baby walker to make progress happen. NHS advice on keeping babies active says lots of time in walkers or bouncers can delay walking, and recommends limiting them if used. The Oxford Health guidance favours safe floor space over baby walkers too.

When should you ask for help?

Ask your health visitor now if at around 12 months your baby is not trying to stand or take weight through their feet, or if something about their movement concerns you. That is the Oxford Health NHS advice for this stage. You can also speak to your GP. If your child is losing a skill they had, seems unusually stiff or floppy, or uses one side of their body much less than the other, seek medical advice promptly rather than waiting to see whether walking appears.

If your child is moving towards standing but still not showing signs of walking by about 15 months, contact your health visitor; that is also the Oxford Health NHS recommendation. If they are not walking without support by 18 months, discuss it with your health visitor or GP. None of these points is a diagnosis. A professional can look at the whole child and decide whether any further assessment would help.

If your baby was born prematurely, tell the health visitor their due date as well as their birth date. Corrected age can matter when discussing development; see our gentle guide to corrected age. Ask for advice tailored to your baby rather than recalculating a deadline alone.

Keep one useful note, not a scorecard

If you are preparing to speak to a health visitor, jot down what your baby does now, what has changed over the past few weeks, and any movement that worries you. A short video of ordinary play can sometimes help you describe what you mean. You do not need a perfect timeline.

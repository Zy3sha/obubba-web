I Have Flu. Can I Still Breastfeed My Baby?

If you have flu, you can usually keep breastfeeding. Here is how to reduce the chance of passing it on, check medicines and get help when either of you is unwell.

breastfeeding with flucan I breastfeed when illflu and breast milk

You are shivery, your baby wants a feed, and suddenly you are wondering whether your milk is safe. If you have flu, you can usually continue breastfeeding. Flu is not passed through breast milk; it mainly spreads through respiratory droplets and contact. Take steps to reduce your baby's exposure, and ask a clinician for advice if you are very unwell or unsure about your particular situation. CDC guidance supports continuing to provide breast milk while taking precautions.

You do not have to manage every feed and every other baby task alone while you feel ill. A partner or carer can take the nappy changes, washing up and settling, and bring your baby to you when it is time to feed.

A simple plan for the next feed

  1. Wash your hands with soap and water before touching your baby or anything they will touch. Wash again after coughing or sneezing into your hands.
  2. Cover coughs and sneezes, bin tissues, and consider wearing a mask while close to your baby if you can do so comfortably. Never put a mask on a baby.
  3. Let someone else do the jobs around the feed if help is available. Ask for water, food and a place to rest once your baby is safely settled.

These steps come from the CDC's flu-and-breastfeeding guidance. They reduce exposure; they cannot make close contact risk-free. If you are too unwell to feed at the breast, the CDC says you can express milk and, if possible, have a well carer give it. Wash hands before expressing and clean pump parts as directed. Ask your midwife, health visitor or breastfeeding supporter for help if illness is making feeding or expressing difficult.

Check the exact cold or flu medicine

Do not assume that an over-the-counter cold-and-flu sachet is safe just because a single ingredient is familiar. Products can combine several medicines. The NHS advises telling a GP, midwife, health visitor or pharmacist that you are breastfeeding and checking cold remedies before taking them. Some decongestants and other medicines are not recommended; the right answer depends on the product and your circumstances. Do not stop a prescribed medicine without professional advice.

If you feel very unwell, have symptoms that worry you, or have a hot, painful breast with flu-like symptoms, contact your GP or NHS 111. A painful breast may need a different assessment: the NHS describes mastitis as causing a hot, painful breast and flu-like feelings. You do not need to work out the cause yourself before asking.

If your baby seems ill too

Keep offering usual feeds, and watch how your baby is feeding and whether they are having wet nappies. If you are worried about symptoms or do not know what to do, call NHS 111. Call 999 or go to A&E if your baby has difficulty breathing, is difficult to wake, or has another emergency sign in the NHS urgent-help guide for under-fives. For a baby under three months, a temperature of 38°C or higher is an emergency sign in that guidance.

Being ill and exhausted can make it easier to doze off during a feed. If you feel sleepy, plan for another adult to take your baby back to their own safe sleep space. The NHS warns never to fall asleep with a sick baby on a sofa.

Keep the handover tiny

If another adult is helping, a short note is enough: when your baby last fed, whether they seemed to feed as usual, and the last wet nappy.

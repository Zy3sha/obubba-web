Formula Feeding Away From Home: What to Pack for One Feed

A calm UK checklist for formula feeds on the go: a fresh powder feed, ready-to-feed carton, clean equipment and what to do when plans change.

The first outing with a bottle can feel like packing for an expedition. The baby is dressed, the bag is by the door—and then you wonder whether the feed can be made now and carried warm.

The simplest safe plan is to make a fresh formula feed when your baby needs it, or take an unopened ready-to-feed liquid carton and a sterilised bottle. For a powder feed away from home, the NHS recommends a measured amount of powder in a clean, dry container, a clean flask of just-boiled hot water, and an empty sterilised bottle with its cap and ring in place. The water must still be hot enough when mixed with the powder. Don't pre-mix a warm bottle in the bag and hope it will be fine later.

Choose one of two packing plans

Plan A: powder, made fresh when needed

  • Pack the powder measured for one feed in a small, clean, dry container.
  • Fill a clean vacuum flask with freshly boiled water and close it. Take an empty sterilised bottle with the cap and retaining ring in place.
  • When your baby shows feeding cues, wash your hands, pour the correct amount of hot water into the bottle first, then add the correct number of scoops of powder. Follow the tin's instructions. The NHS says the water needs to be at least 70°C when it meets the powder, because powdered formula is not sterile; a flask does not guarantee that temperature indefinitely.
  • Cool the closed bottle under cold running water, then test a drop on the inside of your wrist. It should feel warm or cool, not hot.

The full NHS make-up instructions matter more than any shortened bag checklist, especially if this is your first time preparing a feed.

Plan B: unopened ready-to-feed liquid formula

Take an unopened carton of the appropriate infant formula and a sterilised bottle. Ready-to-feed formula is sterile until opened, but the NHS says the feeding equipment still needs sterilising. Follow the carton instructions; once opened, do not treat it as a shelf-stable, resealable drink. If you use only part, the NHS says to keep the unused liquid at the back of the fridge on the top shelf for no longer than 24 hours. On an outing without a fridge, using a small carton that matches the feed avoids that storage problem.

Neither plan needs you to predict the exact minute your baby will be hungry. Watch their cues; the NHS describes early hunger signs such as restlessness, rooting and looking for something to suck. If the trip runs long

If the flask water is no longer hot enough to make a powder feed safely, do not mix the powder with cool water. Use an unopened ready-to-feed carton with sterilised equipment, or find a safe way to make a fresh feed. Bottled water is not a routine shortcut: the NHS says it is not usually recommended because it is not sterile and may contain too much sodium or sulphate.

Taking a made-up feed from home is a fallback, not the everyday plan. If there is no other option, the NHS gives specific cooling and transport limits: cool it promptly, refrigerate it, take it out just before leaving and carry it in a cool bag with an ice pack; use it within 4 hours. Without an ice pack or fridge, the made-up feed must be used within 2 hours. Do not leave it in a warm pram bag or car. If you cannot meet the storage conditions, discard it and make a safe new feed.

After any feed, throw away formula left in the bottle. Do not keep it for the next stop, and do not warm formula in a microwave. For the separate question of cleaning and sterilising equipment, see when you can stop sterilising bottles.

Keep the record smaller than the outing

If another adult will do the next feed, the useful handover is simply what was offered, when, and roughly how much your baby drank—not how much was prepared. This is general UK information, not individual medical advice. If your baby has a prescribed formula or feeding plan, follow your clinician's and the product's instructions. Ask your health visitor or GP about ongoing feeding concerns; seek urgent medical help if your baby is unwell or showing signs of dehydration.

You're Ill—Who Cares for Baby Today?

What to do when the parent who usually knows every feed and nap is ill: a short baby-care handover, safer-sleep checks and a realistic plan for help.

caring for baby when parent is sickbaby care backup planshared baby care

You wake up ill, but the baby still needs feeding, changing and holding. And somehow you're the person who knows when the last bottle was, which song settles them and where the spare sleep bag lives.

Start with the next few hours, not a perfect routine. Ask a trusted adult to take a specific shift if you can. Give them one short handover: what has happened, what the baby needs next, what is important for safety and how to reach you. Rest is a need, not a reward for finishing every baby task.

First, work out what help you actually need

“Can you help today?” can leave both people guessing. Try an ask with a start and end:

“I'm unwell and need to lie down from 10 until 12. Can you take the baby after this feed, change them and settle the next nap? I'll tell you what's happened so far.”

If nobody can come immediately, reduce the day to essentials: feeds, nappies, a safe place for sleep and your own fluids and rest where possible. Chores, outings and the usual nap timetable can wait. If you are too unwell to care safely, contact a trusted adult promptly; ask your GP or NHS 111 for advice about your own illness when needed. In an emergency, call 999.

The five-line handover

Use a note, a text or the baby-care handover template. The receiving adult needs a usable next step, not a complete history:

  1. Last care: “Fed at 9:15; wet nappy at 9:40; woke at 8:50.” Give times only if you know them—do not invent a missing log.
  2. Next likely need: “Offer milk when they show hunger cues; they may be ready for a nap later.” A forecast is not an instruction to delay a feed.
  3. What works today: The usual settling cue, preferred bottle or breast-milk plan, and where supplies are.
  4. Non-negotiables: Any allergy, prescribed medicine or individual care plan, with the written instructions and emergency contact. Do not rely on a remembered dose.
  5. When to call you: What the carer can decide, what should prompt a call, and when you will check in.

Make the sleep part explicit

An ill, exhausted adult can drift off while holding or feeding a baby. Before you rest, agree who is awake and responsible for the baby. If you feel you might fall asleep, place the baby in their own safe sleep space rather than remaining on a sofa or armchair with them.

The NHS safer-sleep advice says the safest place for a baby during the first six months is a cot or Moses basket, on their back, in the same room as a parent. Keep the mattress firm and flat and the sleep space clear. The NHS says never to sleep with a baby on a sofa or armchair; bed-sharing is not safe when you are extremely tired or have taken medication that makes you drowsy. Tell the helping adult the same rules, even if they have cared for babies before.

If you have a cough or cold

You do not need to solve infection control alone. Keep the basics practical: the NHS advises regular handwashing across the family to help stop colds spreading. If you are unsure about close contact, breastfeeding or medicines while ill, ask a pharmacist, midwife, health visitor or GP for advice specific to you and your baby rather than changing a feeding plan from a blog post.

If the baby also becomes unwell, the question has changed from organising a handover to assessing the baby. The NHS fever guidance says to call NHS 111 for a baby under three months with a temperature of 38°C or higher, or a baby aged three to six months with 39°C or higher. Seek help sooner if you are worried, especially if the baby is not feeding as usual, is difficult to wake or has breathing trouble; call 999 for an emergency. A tracker cannot decide that a baby is well.

When the shift ends, close the loop

Ask the carer for just three things: what the baby ate, when they slept and anything unusual. Add the missing facts to your shared record if useful, and let the rest go. A sick day is not a failed routine—it is a day when the plan had to flex.

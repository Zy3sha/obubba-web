Can Visitors Kiss My Newborn? A Kind Way to Say No

It is reasonable to ask visitors not to kiss your newborn. Here is the NHS safety advice, a simple message to send before visits and what to do if your baby seems unwell.

can visitors kiss newbornno kissing baby rulenewborn visitors cold sores

Someone is already leaning in to kiss your newborn, and you find yourself trying to be polite faster than you can think. It is reasonable to ask visitors not to kiss your baby. You can make that the house rule before anyone arrives, without accusing anyone of being careless. Ask people to wash their hands before holding your baby and to postpone the visit if they are unwell.

There is a particular reason to be firm about cold sores. The NHS says the virus that causes cold sores can pass to a baby through a kiss, and advises that anyone with a current cold sore or a recent history of one should not kiss the baby. It says babies may be most at risk of neonatal herpes in the first six weeks. This is a precaution, not a prediction that a particular kiss will make a baby ill.

Send the rule before the doorbell rings

One short message saves a tense moment later:

“We'd love you to meet the baby. Please wash your hands when you arrive, and no kisses for now—even on the hands. If you have a cold, cold sore or feel unwell, let's choose another day. Thank you for helping us keep this simple.”

You can send it to everyone, including your own family, so nobody feels singled out. If a visitor forgets, try: “I'll take them back for a moment. We're doing no kisses with everyone.” Your baby does not need an explanation of the boundary; the adults do.

The NHS neonatal herpes advice says everyone should wash their hands before handling a newborn and discourages kisses from people who are not close family or carers. An NHS maternity campaign also recommends that people who are ill or have recently been ill postpone visiting. You are allowed to set a simpler, stricter no-kisses rule for all visitors if that feels easier to hold.

What about parents or the people caring for the baby?

The NHS says the top of the head is the safest place to kiss a baby, and advises avoiding kisses near the mouth, nose and eyes. If you have a cold sore or recent cold-sore symptoms, do not kiss your baby, even if you are a parent or main carer. Wash your hands before contact and speak to a health professional if you are unsure how to care for your baby safely. This is not a reason to withhold comfort; it is a reason to take the NHS cold-sore precautions seriously.

If a visit becomes an actual care handover—for example, a grandparent will look after your baby while you rest—agree on the usual feeds, naps, nappies and who to call if something changes. If someone has already kissed your baby

Take a breath. You cannot tell from the kiss alone whether an infection has been passed on. If the person had a cold sore, or you are worried about an exposure, contact your midwife, GP or NHS 111 for advice specific to your baby. Do not wait for an app or a symptom checklist to decide for you.

The NHS says to call a GP or NHS 111 straight away if a newborn is unusually irritable and will not settle, is feeding less well, has a temperature of 38°C or higher, or develops a rash or sores on the skin, eyes or inside the mouth. Call 999 if your baby is very listless, has a non-fading rash, has difficulty breathing or is difficult to wake, in line with NHS urgent-help guidance. Symptoms may not look like an adult cold sore.

This is general information, not an assessment of your baby. The aim is not to make every cuddle frightening. It is to let the people who love your baby show that love with clean hands, a postponed visit when needed and respect for your boundary.

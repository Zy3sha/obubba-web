Everyone Has Advice About My Baby. What Can I Say Back?

Short, kind scripts for unwanted baby advice—from harmless suggestions to safety boundaries—without turning every visit into an argument.

unsolicited baby advicesetting boundaries with family after babywhat to say to unwanted parenting advice

“We always did it this way.” It may be said with love. It can still be the fifth piece of advice you have heard before lunch, when what you really needed was someone to make you a cup of tea.

You can thank someone for caring without agreeing to follow their advice. A short answer is often enough: “Thanks for thinking of us. We're doing it this way.” If they will care for your baby, be more specific about the instructions they must follow. You do not owe a debate at every visit.

The NHS notes that relationships with family and friends can change after a baby. It recommends being clear about the help you want, because relatives cannot always guess. That clarity can include what you do not want—another tip, a changed routine or a visitor taking over.

Pick the smallest answer that fits

You do not need one perfect speech. Try the level that matches the moment:

  • A passing suggestion: “Thank you. We'll keep that in mind.” You can move on without promising to use it.
  • The same advice again: “I know you mean well. We've made our decision, so let's leave this one there.”
  • Advice when you need practical help: “Could you hold the baby while I eat instead? That would help me more today.”
  • A comment that stings: “I feel judged when my feeding choices are questioned. Please don't bring that up again.”
  • A visit that is too much: “We're keeping today quiet. Let's arrange another time.”

You can say these gently, by text, or with a partner speaking alongside you. If a long explanation invites another long argument, repeat the short answer. A boundary is a statement of what you will do, not a challenge to win.

Preferences and safety are different conversations

Some advice is about preference: which lullaby, which bib, whether you log every nap. You can let a harmless idea pass.

Other advice affects safety. For example, if someone suggests putting a young baby on their front to sleep, be direct: “We put the baby on their back in a clear, flat sleep space. Please follow that every time.” This reflects current NHS safer-sleep guidance, not a parenting trend. If a person cannot agree to an essential safety instruction, do not leave them in charge of that care. They may still be able to help with shopping or washing-up.

The same principle applies to any individual medical or feeding plan: give the carer the current instructions from your clinician or product label, rather than asking them to remember what you discussed at the door. When it is someone you love

It can feel harder with a parent, friend or partner because you want the relationship too. Try naming both truths: “I know you want to help. I also need you to trust that we've thought about this.” Then offer a role that really helps: “If you can bring lunch, we'd love that.”

If you and a partner disagree, talk privately about the actual decision before explaining it to wider family. You do not have to present a united performance when you still need a conversation, but you can ask relatives not to referee it. The NHS encourages partners to say clearly what they need and what is bothering them. If the comments are wearing you down

Feeling hurt by constant criticism does not mean you are too sensitive. You can shorten visits, ask another adult to field messages, or choose one person who will listen without offering solutions. If low mood or anxiety continues, worsens or makes coping hard, talk to a GP, midwife or health visitor; NHS postnatal-depression guidance says you can seek help even if you have only some symptoms. If someone's behaviour feels controlling or frightening, the NHS relationships guide also points to domestic-abuse support. In immediate danger, call 999.

Baby Only Settles for One Parent at Bedtime? Try a Gentle Handover

When your baby wants one parent at bedtime, start with familiar care, a small handover and a safe sleep plan—without turning tears into a test.

Your partner has the same pyjamas, the same dim room and the same lullaby. Your baby is still asking very loudly for you. It can feel as if one parent is the only person who knows how to do bedtime—and the other has somehow failed.

A preference for one familiar person is not a verdict on either parent. Start by making the second parent part of calm, ordinary care, then hand over one small part of bedtime while both adults stay responsive. Keep feeds and safe sleep in the plan. The aim is not a tear-free performance tonight; it is a bedtime both of you can gradually understand and share.

First, ask what the baby is actually asking for

Is a feed due? Is your baby unwell, uncomfortable or overtired? Does the upset begin when one parent leaves, or only when a particular settling step changes? You do not need to diagnose the cry, but those details change the next sensible move.

For older babies, separation can be part of the picture. The NHS says separation anxiety is common from around six months to three years. It suggests practising short separations with someone your baby knows, rather than treating a long, difficult goodbye as the only way to learn. A newborn who needs frequent feeds is in a different situation: protect those feeds and follow any individual feeding advice.

Try a four-step handover, not a sudden switch

  1. Build familiarity outside bedtime. Let the second parent do a pleasant, low-pressure part of the day: a cuddle after a feed, a walk, a nappy change or a book. Bedtime need not be their first solo hour.
  2. Keep a recognisable closing sequence. A fresh nappy, lower lights, a short book or song and a cuddle are enough. The NHS says a simple, soothing bedtime routine may help babies settle. It does not require both adults to sound or move exactly alike.
  3. Pass over one step while the familiar parent is nearby. For example, one parent finishes a feed and the other sings and cuddles before placing baby down. Next time, the second parent might begin the book too. If your baby is very upset, pause, comfort them and try a smaller step another day. You are allowed to change the plan.
  4. Share the useful facts, then let the settling parent respond. Say when the last feed and nappy happened, what usually helps, and whether anything unusual is going on. Do not direct every movement from the doorway. A calm handover is more useful than two adults arguing about technique over a tired baby.

This is a way to practise, not a sleep-training timetable or a promise that bedtime will improve in a set number of nights. If breastfeeding is part of settling, sharing bedtime does not require dropping a needed feed. One parent can feed and the other can take the next comfortable step. Keep the sleep space safe, whoever does the settling

If your baby falls asleep in someone's arms, the adult needs to stay awake and move them to their own clear, flat sleep space. Put baby down on their back; for at least the first six months, keep that space in the same room as an adult for day and night sleep. Those are NHS safer-sleep recommendations. A parent's scented scarf, toy or loose blanket must not go into a baby's sleep space, even if it seems comforting during separation.

If the adult is so exhausted they might doze while holding or feeding baby, stop and make a safer plan before continuing. You can trade places, take a brief reset, or ask another trusted adult for help. The handover is working when everyone can respond safely—not when one person pushes through exhaustion.

What to keep in one shared note

Try three lines: “Last feed 7:10pm; nappy changed. Calms with the low song and a slow walk. Congested today, so please tell me if breathing or feeding seems different.” Replace that example with what is true for your baby. The note should help the next carer, not become a score of which parent settled them fastest.

If separation distress is prolonged or is making day-to-day care very hard, the NHS advises speaking to your health visitor. Seek medical advice sooner if your baby seems ill, feeds much less than usual or you are worried about their breathing. This is general information, not an assessment of your baby.
